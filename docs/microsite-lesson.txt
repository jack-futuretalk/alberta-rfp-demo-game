Food Literacy Through Advertising Analysis
Introduction
In this lesson, students examine the less obvious methods advertisers use to reach consumers,
with a particular focus on how food and beverage brands use "soft sell" advertising to shape
how teens think about nutrition and growth. Students first learn to tell soft sell ads (which build a
feeling about a brand) apart from hard sell ads (which make direct claims about a product).
They then apply this thinking specifically to food marketing, learning how to separate a brand's
feeling from a food's actual nutritional value, and how to find and trust credible sources of
nutrition information instead.
Learning Objectives
What Is Advertising Actually Trying to Do?
Before students can spot a soft sell ad, they need to be clear on what all advertising is for in the
first place: getting someone to like or buy something. This section resets that foundation before
moving into the subtler techniques.
Class Discussion: The Purpose of Advertising
Open with the question: What is the purpose of advertising? Let students discuss for a few
minutes, making sure the idea surfaces that ads exist to sell products or services — even when
they don't look like a sales pitch.
Key Points:
1. Explain that the purpose of advertising is to get you to like or buy something
2. Recognize that advertising uses different techniques and appeals for different products
3. Identify how advertisers use different media, approaches, and techniques to reach different
audiences
4. Find, identify, and analyze examples of "soft sell" advertising techniques
5. Communicate your findings to the class
6. Identify credible sources for nutritional information (Alberta PDE8 curriculum outcome)
7. Explore ways nutrition supports growth and development (Alberta PDE8 curriculum
outcome)

Hard Sell vs. Soft Sell
Introduce the distinction: a hard sell ad makes direct claims about a product (price, features,
"buy now"). A soft sell ad makes people know and like a brand without saying much about the
product at all.
Key Points:
Quiz
A food advertisement showing bright colours, upbeat music, and friends laughing together is
an example of a soft sell technique because it subtly creates a positive mood instead of
making direct claims about the product’s nutrition.
Answer: True

Identifying Credible Sources for Nutritional Information
Soft sell ads work partly because they replace facts with feeling — which makes it easy for a
brand's personality to stand in for real nutritional information. This section teaches students how
to tell the difference between a brand telling a story and a source giving them accurate,
evidence-based nutrition guidance.
Brand Voice vs. Credible Source
Discuss: when a snack or drink brand's ad or social account talks about "fueling your body" or
"clean ingredients," is that a nutrition claim from a credible source, or a marketing message?
Introduce the idea that a brand's own marketing (packaging, ads, sponsored influencer posts) is
never a neutral source of nutrition information, even when it sounds scientific.
Key Points:
Ads create warmth, energy, and connection
Focus on emotional message, not claims
Product placement — paying to have a product featured in a show, movie, or game
Endorsement — paying a real person to be associated with a product
Identity and emotion — connecting a brand to something important to the audience's
identity or emotions
Parody — flattering the audience by poking fun at other ad techniques

They often include prices and urgency

How Food Marketing Connects to Growth and Development
This section links the media-literacy skills from Sections 1–3 to the practical question of why
accurate nutrition information matters at this age: adolescence is a period of rapid physical and
cognitive growth, and food marketing is deliberately aimed at teens during exactly this window.
Why Nutrition Matters More During the Teen Years
Briefly explain that adolescence involves a growth spurt and significant physical and brain
development, which increases the body's need for adequate, balanced nutrition (protein, iron,
calcium, and overall energy intake, among other things). This is why teens are such a heavily
targeted advertising audience for food and beverage brands.
Key Points:
Spotting Soft Sell in Food & Drink Marketing
Return to the four techniques from Section 2, but now applied specifically to food and beverage
brands: a sports drink using an athlete endorsement, a cereal using a cartoon mascot
placement, a snack brand's "identity" ad tied to friendship or belonging, or a "healthy soda"
brand parodying diet-culture advertising.
Key Points:
Feeling vs. Fact Check
As a quick group activity, take one food or drink ad from 4.2 and have students identify (a) what
feeling the ad is selling, and (b) what a credible source would actually say about that product's
nutritional role, using the criteria from Section 3.2.
Key Points:
Credible nutrition sources are trustworthy and well made
Check the creator, source, date, and evidence
Use trusted health groups and Nutrition Facts labels
Compare claims with sugar, fibre, and serving size
Test soft-sell claims against nutrition facts
Check ingredients and trusted health sources
Healthy image may not match nutrition

Activity — Alternate Ad Audit (Nutrition Edition)
Now that students can name the four soft-sell techniques and know how to check a claim
against a credible source, this activity has them put both skills to work independently. Working
in groups, students go out and find real examples in the wild, analyze them using the same
questions from Sections 1–4, and — for the first time — check a food-related ad's impression
against an actual credible nutrition source. This is where the lesson moves from recognizing
techniques to applying them critically.
Group Assignment
Divide the class into four groups (or eight, depending on class size). Assign each group one of
the four soft-selling techniques (product placement, endorsement, identity appeal, parody).
Each group finds four ads using their technique — at least one of which must be for a food,
beverage, or supplement product.
Key Points:

Summary
Summary & Key Takeaways
You now understand that advertising is never neutral — even ads that make no claims about a
product at all are still working to build a feeling about a brand, and food and beverage
marketing is no exception. You can name the four soft-sell techniques, tell a brand's marketing
voice apart from a credible nutrition source, and explain why teens are such a heavily targeted
audience during a period of significant growth and development.
Advertising's core purpose is always to get someone to like or buy something — even when
it doesn't look like a traditional ad.
Soft sell ads build a feeling about a brand rather than making claims about a product, using
product placement, endorsement, identity/emotion, or parody.
A brand's own marketing is never a neutral source of nutrition information, no matter how
scientific or wellness-focused it sounds.
Credible nutrition sources are produced by recognized health authorities or regulated
professionals, are free of a commercial motive, and are consistent with other trustworthy
sources.
Adolescence is a period of significant physical and cognitive growth, which is exactly why
teens are such a heavily targeted audience for food and beverage advertising — and
exactly why being able to check marketing claims against credible sources matters.
Media-literate consumers can separate the feeling an ad sells from the facts a credible
source provides — for food marketing just as much as any other product.
